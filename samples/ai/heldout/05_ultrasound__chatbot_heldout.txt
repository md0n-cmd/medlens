There is also a mention of possible early gallbladder inflammation, which deserves attention.

Let me explain each finding in simple language.

# 1\. Liver – Enlarged with Grade I fatty liver

Your report says:

- Liver size: 16.5 cm.
- Diffusely increased echogenicity.
- Impression: Hepatomegaly with Grade I fatty liver.

### What does this mean?

**Hepatomegaly** means the liver is slightly enlarged.

**Grade I fatty liver** means a mild amount of fat has accumulated inside the liver cells.

In simple terms, the liver is storing more fat than it normally should.

Common contributing factors include:

- Being overweight or having abdominal fat.
- High cholesterol or triglycerides.
- Diabetes or insulin resistance.
- Regular alcohol consumption.
- Sedentary lifestyle.
- Certain medications.

### Is Grade I fatty liver dangerous?

Usually, Grade I fatty liver is an early finding.

The good news is that fatty liver can often improve significantly with lifestyle changes.

### What should be done?

I would recommend discussing these tests with your doctor:

- Liver Function Test (LFT).
- Lipid profile.
- Fasting blood sugar.
- HbA1c.
- Blood pressure and weight assessment.

Lifestyle recommendations:

- Reduce sugary drinks and sweets.
- Reduce fried and highly processed foods.
- Exercise for at least 150 minutes weekly.
- Maintain a healthy body weight.
- Avoid alcohol, particularly while the liver is being evaluated.

# 2\. Gallbladder – 6 mm gallstone

This is the second important finding.

Your report says:

"A 6 mm echogenic focus is seen, likely a calculus."

### What does this mean?

A calculus is another word for a stone.

There is a small stone measuring approximately **6 mm inside the gallbladder**.

This condition is called **cholelithiasis (gallstones)**.

Cholelithiasis

Gallstones form when substances in bile, such as cholesterol, crystallise and form stones.

### Can a 6 mm gallstone cause problems?

Yes, even a small stone can cause symptoms.

Possible symptoms include:

- Pain in the upper right side of the abdomen.
- Pain after eating oily or fatty foods.
- Pain extending towards the right shoulder or back.
- Nausea.
- Vomiting.
- Abdominal discomfort.

Some people have gallstones without experiencing any symptoms.

### What about early cholecystitis?

Your radiologist has written:

"? Early cholecystitis cannot be ruled out."

This is important.

Cholecystitis  means inflammation of the gallbladder.

It can happen when a gallstone obstructs the gallbladder's outlet.

Symptoms can include:

- Persistent upper-right abdominal pain.
- Fever.
- Nausea and vomiting.
- Tenderness over the gallbladder area.

**If the patient currently has persistent abdominal pain, fever or vomiting, they should seek same-day medical assessment.** If the pain is severe, or there is jaundice, seek emergency care.

The ultrasound alone does not confirm cholecystitis. A doctor needs to correlate the findings with symptoms, examination and blood tests.

# 4\. What investigations should be done next?

I would suggest discussing the following with a physician or gastroenterologist:

| Investigation | Why it is useful |
| --- | --- |
| LFT | Checks liver enzymes and bilirubin |
| CBC | Looks for infection or inflammation |
| CRP | Helps assess inflammation |
| Lipid profile | Checks cholesterol and triglycerides |
| HbA1c | Checks average blood sugar |
| Fasting blood sugar | Checks glucose levels |

Depending on symptoms and examination, the doctor may recommend further gallbladder evaluation or a surgical consultation.

If acute cholecystitis is confirmed, treatment may involve hospital care and, in many cases, laparoscopic gallbladder removal.

Importantly, an asymptomatic gallstone does not automatically require surgery.
